**In short**: sudoku is not an ancient Japanese tradition. It was first published in 1979 in the United States under the name "Number Place," picked up and renamed "Sudoku" by the Japanese publisher Nikoli in 1984, then went global from 2004-2005 onward thanks to New Zealand judge Wayne Gould and the British newspaper *The Times*.

Many players assume sudoku is a centuries-old Japanese tradition. In reality, it's an American puzzle, popularized in Japan, that became a worldwide phenomenon in just a few years around the turn of the 2000s. Its history is much shorter — and much more interesting — than most people think.

## A mathematical ancestor: Latin squares

The basic idea behind sudoku — filling a grid so that a given symbol never appears twice in the same row or column — is nothing new. It rests on a mathematical object called a **Latin square**, studied as early as the 18th century by Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler. A Latin square is a square grid filled with symbols (numbers, for instance) where each symbol appears exactly once per row and once per column.

Euler notably worked on a famous problem, the "36 officers problem": can 36 officers of 6 different ranks and 6 different regiments be arranged in a 6×6 grid so that no row or column contains the same rank or regiment twice? He proved it was impossible for that specific case — but the concept of the Latin square would go on to survive well beyond pure mathematics, eventually becoming the logical foundation of sudoku two centuries later.

## The birth of modern sudoku, in the United States

Sudoku as we know it today — a 9×9 grid divided into nine 3×3 blocks, with the extra constraint that each block must also contain every digit from 1 to 9 exactly once — first appears in 1979, in the American magazine *Dell Pencil Puzzles and Word Games*. It was published under the name **"Number Place,"** with no author officially credited at the time, though it's generally attributed to **Howard Garns**, a retired architect and freelance puzzle creator.

The concept caught on, but stayed relatively niche in the United States for several years — just one puzzle among many in the pages of puzzle magazines.

## The move to Japan, and the birth of the name "Sudoku"

In 1984, the Japanese publisher **Nikoli**, which specializes in logic puzzles, discovered "Number Place" and decided to publish it in its own magazines. That's where it got its current name: **Sūji wa dokushin ni kagiru** (数字は独身に限る), literally "the digits must remain single" — meaning, alone, without duplicates. The name was later shortened to **Sudoku** (数独), a contraction of *sūji* (digit) and *dokushin* (single/celibate).

Nikoli also introduced a decisive improvement: limiting the number of starting clues (generally no more than 30 out of 81 cells) and arranging them symmetrically, to make each grid more elegant and more interesting to solve. It's this "Nikoli-style" version that would become the worldwide standard.

## Going global, thanks to a New Zealand judge

Sudoku remained an essentially Japanese game until the early 2000s. The turning point came thanks to **Wayne Gould**, a retired New Zealand judge who discovered the game during a trip to Japan in 1997. Fascinated, he spent the next six years developing a computer program capable of automatically generating valid sudoku grids with controlled difficulty.

In 2004, he managed to convince the British newspaper **The Times** to publish a sudoku puzzle every day. The success was immediate and spectacular: other British newspapers, then international ones, followed suit within months. **2005** is generally considered the tipping-point year: sudoku became a global phenomenon, published daily in hundreds of newspapers, and spun off into books, video games, and later mobile apps.

## And today?

Since then, sudoku has never really left popular culture. World championships have existed since 2006 (the **World Sudoku Championship**), gathering solvers capable of finishing a hard grid in just over a minute. The game has also spawned numerous variants — diagonal sudoku, killer sudoku, irregular-region sudoku — that carry on the same basic logical principle, forty years after its first publication in an American magazine.

From a Latin square studied by Euler to a daily game played by millions of people worldwide, sudoku is one of the rare examples of a mathematical puzzle turned global popular culture — and it still occupies, every day, the same 81 cells it did in 1979.
