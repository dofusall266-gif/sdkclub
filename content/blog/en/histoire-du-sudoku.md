**In short**: sudoku is not an ancient Japanese tradition. It was first published in 1979 in the United States under the name "Number Place", picked up and renamed "Sudoku" by the Japanese publisher Nikoli in 1984, then went global from 2004-2005 thanks to New Zealand judge Wayne Gould and the British daily *The Times*.

Many players think sudoku is a centuries-old Japanese tradition. In reality, it is an American puzzle, popularized in Japan, that became a worldwide phenomenon in just a few years around the turn of the 2000s. Its history is much shorter — and much more interesting — than you might imagine.

## A mathematical ancestor: Latin squares

The basic idea of sudoku — filling a grid so that the same symbol never appears twice in a row or column — is nothing new. It rests on a mathematical object called a **Latin square**, studied as early as the 18th century by the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler. A Latin square is a square grid filled with symbols (numbers, for example) in which each symbol appears exactly once per row and once per column.

Euler took a particular interest in a famous problem, the "36 officers problem": can 36 officers of 6 different ranks and 6 different regiments be arranged in a 6×6 grid so that no row or column contains the same rank or the same regiment twice? He showed it was impossible for this specific case — but the concept of the Latin square would survive well beyond pure mathematics, eventually becoming the logical basis of sudoku two centuries later.

## The birth of modern sudoku, in the United States

Sudoku as we know it — a 9×9 grid divided into nine 3×3 blocks, with the extra constraint that each block must also contain each digit from 1 to 9 once — first appeared in 1979 in the American magazine *Dell Pencil Puzzles and Word Games*. It was published there under the name **"Number Place"**, with no author officially credited at the time, but generally attributed to **Howard Garns**, a retired architect and freelance puzzle creator.

The principle caught on, but remained relatively obscure in the United States for several years — one game among others in the pages of puzzle magazines.

## The move to Japan, and the birth of the name "Sudoku"

In 1984, the Japanese publishing house **Nikoli**, which specializes in logic puzzles, discovered "Number Place" and decided to publish it in its own magazines. That is where it received its current name: **Sūji wa dokushin ni kagiru** (数字は独身に限る), literally "the numbers must remain single" — meaning alone, with no duplicates. The name was then shortened to **Sudoku** (数独), a contraction of *sūji* (number) and *dokushin* (single).

Nikoli also made a decisive improvement: limiting the number of starting clues (generally no more than 30 out of 81 cells) and arranging them symmetrically, to make each grid more elegant and more interesting to solve. It is this "Nikoli-style" version that would become the worldwide standard.

## The worldwide boom, thanks to a New Zealand judge

Sudoku remained an essentially Japanese game until the early 2000s. The turning point came thanks to **Wayne Gould**, a retired New Zealand judge who discovered the game on a trip to Japan in 1997. Fascinated, he spent the following six years developing a computer program capable of automatically generating valid sudoku grids with controlled difficulty.

In 2004, he managed to convince the British daily **The Times** to publish a sudoku grid every day. The success was immediate and spectacular: other British newspapers, then international ones, followed suit within just a few months. **2005** is generally regarded as the tipping point: sudoku became a global phenomenon, published daily in hundreds of newspapers, and spun off into books, video games, and then mobile apps.

## And today?

Since then, sudoku has never really left popular culture. World championships have existed since 2006 (the **World Sudoku Championship**), bringing together solvers able to finish a hard grid in barely more than a minute. The game has also given rise to numerous variants — diagonal sudoku, killer sudoku, irregular-region sudoku — which carry on the same basic logical principle, forty years after its first publication in an American magazine.

From a Latin square studied by Euler to a daily game for millions of people around the world, sudoku is one of the rare examples of a mathematical puzzle that became global popular culture — and every day it still fills the same 81 cells as in 1979.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is sudoku really Japanese?**
No. The principle was first published in the United States in 1979, under the name "Number Place". Japan gave it its current name in 1984 and a refined version, but the origin is American.

**Who invented sudoku?**
The modern 9×9 format is generally attributed to Howard Garns, a retired architect, in the American magazine *Dell Pencil Puzzles and Word Games* in 1979.

**Since when has sudoku been popular worldwide?**
Since 2004-2005, when Wayne Gould convinced the British daily *The Times* to publish a daily grid — the trigger for its worldwide spread.
