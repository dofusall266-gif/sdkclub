**In short**: sudoku genuinely engages working memory, attention and logical reasoning *while* you play — that is well established scientifically. However, the evidence that it durably boosts general intelligence or prevents Alzheimer's disease is weak and unproven; it is mostly overall lifestyle habits (sleep, physical activity, social life) that protect cognitive health in the long run.

"Sudoku works out your brain", "a shield against Alzheimer's disease"... These lines come up again and again in articles about the benefits of sudoku. Some are well founded, others much less so. Rather than sell you a miracle promise, we'd rather take an honest look at what research actually shows — and where its limits lie.

*What follows is a summary of general information and in no way replaces medical advice. For any personal question about cognitive health, talk to a healthcare professional.*

## What is well established: real cognitive work, in the moment

Solving a sudoku grid actively engages several cognitive functions, and this is solidly documented:

- **Working memory** — keeping several possible candidates for a cell in mind, or remembering what you have already eliminated in a row, directly draws on this short-term memory.
- **Sustained attention** — a hard grid requires you to stay focused on one task for several minutes in a row, without getting distracted.
- **Deductive reasoning and pattern recognition** — every solving technique (naked pairs, X-Wing, etc.) is an exercise in pure logic, where you learn to spot recurring structures.

These effects are real, measurable, and happen **while** you play. This is where the scientific consensus is strongest.

## What is more debated: does it durably "work out" the brain?

This is where things get complicated. The idea that training on a game like sudoku would improve cognitive abilities *in general* — beyond sudoku itself — is known as the **cognitive transfer** hypothesis. Yet a large part of the research on the subject (notably large meta-analyses published in well-regarded scientific journals, as well as an open letter signed by several dozen cognitive neuroscience researchers in 2014) converges on a more nuanced conclusion than what "brain training" app marketing claims: you undeniably get better at **the game you practice**, but the evidence for transfer to general intelligence, everyday memory or other cognitive tasks is weak and inconsistent across studies.

In other words: sudoku will probably make you better... at sudoku. That is already a worthwhile goal in itself, but it's more honest not to promise more.

## And what about preventing Alzheimer's disease?

This is probably the most widespread claim — and the most scientifically fragile. Several observational studies have indeed found an **association** between regularly doing intellectually stimulating activities (crosswords, sudoku, reading, board games) and a reduced risk of cognitive decline in older adults. But an association is not proof of cause: it is entirely possible that it works the other way around — that people whose cognitive abilities are already well preserved are simply more inclined to keep doing this kind of activity (a phenomenon called "reverse causation"), rather than the activity itself protecting the brain.

To date, major health agencies and the scientific literature mostly agree that it is **overall lifestyle habits** — regular physical activity, good sleep, a balanced diet, an active social life, stress management — that have the strongest evidence for long-term cognitive health. Sudoku can fit perfectly well into that overall healthy lifestyle, but it doesn't replace it.

## An underrated benefit: relaxation, not performance

There is, however, a less often highlighted effect that is just as valuable: the **state of mental absorption** that a grid well matched to your level provides. That moment when your mind focuses entirely on a structured task, with a clear goal and visible progress, corresponds to what psychologists call a "flow" state. It is a genuine everyday stress-management tool — a mental break, away from notifications and endless scrolling — regardless of any long-term effect on the brain.

## In summary

Sudoku is an excellent exercise in concentration and logic, in the moment, and a legitimate relaxing activity to work into your routine. On the other hand, be wary of promises of disease prevention or general intelligence gains: science isn't yet there to confirm them so simply. Play for the pleasure of logic and the break it gives you — that's already an excellent reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does sudoku prevent Alzheimer's disease?**
It hasn't been shown to. Observational studies associate stimulating activities with healthy cognitive aging, but an association is not proof of cause — the reverse (reverse causation) remains possible.

**Does sudoku make you smarter?**
Not in general, no. Research mostly shows that you get better **at sudoku itself**; the evidence for transfer to other cognitive abilities is weak.

**So what is the real benefit?**
A real, structured mental break ("flow" state) and genuine working on working memory, attention and logic while you play — already a good reason to spend a few minutes on it. [Try it for yourself](/jouer).
